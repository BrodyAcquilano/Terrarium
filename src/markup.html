This is the simplest terrarium model. A heating panel on top, and one variable: temperature. The terrarium is empty and sealed, with just air inside — and the job is to model how the heat distributes through that air.

The simplifications, in order, before we begin. This model rests on seven simplifications, listed here in the order the shorthand block diagram in section 11 uses them — and each one is mentioned again where it happens in the note below.

  1. The heat is assumed to go straight down, and the whole terrarium is taken to be heated this way: changes in the other two directions are ignored, so the temperature varies only with depth down the tank.
  2. The pad temperature is set equal to the slider setting at steady state: TH = Ts.
  3. A set of particles becomes a Boltzmann distribution (made before the diagram even starts, and carried through the whole chain).
  4. That distribution becomes a single average temperature, by the integral average.
  5. The pad temperature is taken as constant in space: T(t, x, y) becomes TH(t), the position parameters x and y removed, because the pad is assumed the same temperature everywhere.
  6. Space is brought back in a simplified form only: attenuation α(x), instead of the full spatial field that simplification 5 removed.
  7. The transient is ignored and only the steady state is kept (the steady-state filter), but only when that is applicable — when a variable, here temperature, is held constant long enough, t ≥ ts.

1. How the heating pad works

The pad converts electrical energy into heat: current pushed through a resistive element (Joule heating) warms the panel, and the thermostat holds its surface at the setpoint temperature, Theating pad — the temperature you set with the slider, also written Ts in the simple solution. We assume the heat is spread uniformly over the panel surface, and from that surface it radiates and conducts downward into the air. For now we follow the heat in one direction only — straight down. The pad is inset from the edges of the terrarium, so a full picture would need heating in three dimensions; that is left for later (section 8).

Simplification 1 — Heat goes straight down only

We assume the heat goes straight down, and that the whole terrarium just gets heated this way. Changes in the other two directions are ignored: sideways spread does not enter the model at all, so as the heat travels, the temperature varies only with depth down the tank — one direction, not three.

2. Particles first — temperature is motion

The air is a huge number of tiny particles, all moving and bouncing — off the glass, off the panel, off each other. Temperature is average particle motion: hotter air just means its particles are, on average, moving faster.

(1/2)m⟨v2⟩ = (3/2) kB T

When a slower air particle bounces off the hot panel surface, it tends to leave faster than it arrived — the bounce hands it energy. Collisions then share that extra motion through the tank. Heat entering the tank is this: faster bounces at the top, spreading downward.

3. The Boltzmann distribution

Not every particle moves at the average speed. At any instant some are nearly still, most are in the middle, a few are racing. Temperature fixes the shape of the spread of speeds — the Boltzmann distribution. The chance of finding a particle with energy E falls off exponentially:

P(E) ∝ e−E / (kB T)

Raise T and the spread shifts to higher speeds and broadens — the fast tail grows.

Energies — Boltzmann distribution probability density vs particle energy

The Boltzmann distribution at each setpoint — same colours as the distribution graphs at the top of the tab, current setpoint bold. Left: how particle speeds are spread out. Few particles are near still, most sit around the peak, and a long fast tail runs off to the right; the dashed line marks the average speed, ⟨v⟩. Right: the same spread by energy — the chance of a particle carrying energy E falls off as e−E/(kB T), and the dashed line marks the average energy, (3/2)kB T. Raise T and both curves shift right, broaden and flatten, and the fast / high-energy tail grows — that tail is the part of the swarm in the particle diagram above drawn orange and red.

What “the average” means — it is an integral. When we say we take the average of the Boltzmann distribution, we do not read a single value off the curve. We add up every energy a particle could have, each one weighted by how likely the distribution says it is, over the whole curve — and that weighted sum is an integral. The brackets ⟨ ⟩ in this note mean exactly that integral average:

⟨E⟩ = ∫0∞ E · P(E) dE = (3/2) kB T

The distribution is normalised — its weights add to one, ∫ P(E) dE = 1 — so ⟨E⟩ is the balance point of the whole curve: every part of it counts, in proportion to its weight. The same integral over the speed curve gives ⟨v2⟩, and it is that, through (1/2)m⟨v2⟩ = ⟨E⟩ in section 2, that sets T. So yes: the average energy is got by integrating, and it is the energy of that average — the mean energy itself — that we then use. The whole spread goes in; one number, ⟨E⟩, comes out; and that number is the temperature.

Not the peak, not the midpoint, not the maximum. The peak of the speed curve is only the most probable speed — the single speed with the most particles near it — and it is not the average: on the left-hand graph the dashed ⟨v⟩ line sits to the right of the peak, because the long fast tail pulls the integral average up even though few particles live out there. There is no midpoint to take instead: the energy curve starts at 0 and tails off without ever quite ending, so it has no middle value — and no maximum either; the tail just gets thinner and thinner. And because energy goes as v2, that tail counts for more than its particle count suggests. One more consequence of averaging by integral: what we average is the energies, so ⟨E⟩ = (1/2)m⟨v2⟩ — which is not the energy of the average speed, (1/2)m⟨v⟩2. That comes out smaller, because averaging first and squaring afterwards throws away exactly the spread the integral keeps. Average the energies first — then the average energy is the temperature. The peak and the maximum never enter it.

A spread over particles, not a swing in time. These are two different things, at two different steps, and they are easy to mix up. The Boltzmann average is taken across particles, at one instant: an enormous number of different energies collapse, by the integral, into the one number T. Only afterwards does that one number vary in time, closing in on its setpoint while the tank settles, as the live graph at the top of this tab shows. The fact that some particles are faster than the average does not make the average swing: each fast particle is fast in a random direction, and within a fraction of a nanosecond the next collision scrambles it again. That is fluctuation — random, unstructured noise — not oscillation, which needs the whole system to move together, in step. And the noise in the average shrinks as 1/√N: with around 1022 particles in the tank, the average temperature jitters by roughly one part in 1011 — far too small to see. Watch a single point in the tank and the particles passing through it would show random speeds, but no predictable, repeating signal: you could draw a clean oscillation through that noise, but it would not be the physics.

The Fourier comparison. The comparison to Fourier is close, and it is worth saying exactly where it holds. A Boltzmann distribution and a frequency spectrum are the same kind of object: a curve that says how much weight sits at each value of something — here, how many particles at each energy; in Fourier's picture, how much of a signal at each frequency. And energy and frequency are genuinely linked — for a wave, E grows in proportion to f, and for light the link is exact, E = hf — so an energy distribution can fairly be read as a kind of spectrum. To rebuild a time-domain signal from a spectrum you use all of it: every frequency component added back together, each with its weight — again an integral, over frequencies instead of energies. Simplification 4 below does the opposite job with the same tool: instead of keeping the whole spectrum in order to rebuild something from it, it uses the integral to collapse the whole distribution down to its one weighted average, ⟨E⟩, and throws the shape of the spread away. One number survives — the average energy, not the maximum, not the peak — and that number is what we call the temperature and feed to everything that follows.

Simplification 3 — Particles → Boltzmann distribution

Instead of tracking every particle and every bounce, we replace the whole set of particles with one Boltzmann distribution: a smooth curve for how the speeds are spread out.

Simplification 4 — Boltzmann distribution → Average temperature

From that whole spread we then take just the average — the average kinetic energy — and that average is the temperature: one number, T, standing in for the entire distribution. And “average” here means the integral average worked out above, ⟨E⟩ = ∫ E · P(E) dE — the whole curve collapsed to its weighted mean energy — not its peak, and not a midpoint read off it.

4. Newton's law of cooling

Now the exchange between the pad and the air. The intuition behind Newton's law of cooling is that the rate of heat exchange is proportional to the temperature difference — the bigger the gap between pad and air, the faster heat flows — times a constant that bundles up the contact area, the air's mass and its heat capacity:

rate of heat exchange ∝ (Theating pad − T)

5. The differential equation, solved — first order, like an RC circuit

Written as a differential equation for the air temperature T, with Ts the setpoint (the pad temperature):

dT/dt = k·(Ts − T)

Solved, it gives the transient you see in the live graph above:

T(t) = Ts − (Ts − T0)·e−kt

Fast change while the gap is big, slowing as the air closes in on the pad temperature, settling exponentially. This holds in both directions — heating up and cooling down are the same gap closing, from opposite sides.

It is an RC circuit. This equation is exactly the equation of a capacitor charging through a resistor. Line the two up term by term:

  • Temperature ↔ voltage. T plays the part of the capacitor voltage V.
  • Heat flow ↔ current. Heat flows from hot to cold the way current flows from high to low voltage.
  • Heat capacity ↔ capacitance. The air's heat capacity, C = m cp ≈ 65 J/K, is the capacitor: it stores energy, and its temperature rises as it fills.
  • Thermal resistance ↔ resistance. How hard it is for heat to pass from the pad into the air, Rth, is the resistor.
  • The pad at Ts ↔ the source voltage Vs. The thermostat holds the pad at the setpoint, the way a supply holds its voltage. Moving the slider is flipping the switch to a new supply voltage.

RC dV/dt = Vs − V   ↔   RthC dT/dt = Ts − T,   so   k = 1/τ = 1/(RthC)

The live graph at the top of this tab is a step response — the time response after a sudden change in the input — and for an RC circuit the step response is this same exponential: 63% of the way after one time constant τ, about 98% after 4τ.

Why it is first order. The order of a system is the number of independent places it can store energy. The air in a sealed, ideal container has exactly one: its heat capacity. One store means one state variable, T, and one first-order differential equation. Its characteristic equation has a single root, s = −1/τ — real and negative — and a single real root can only give a decaying exponential. There is nothing in it that can produce a sine wave.

Why it does not ring. To ring — to overshoot the target and swing back — a system needs two different kinds of energy store that hand energy back and forth. In a circuit those are a capacitor (energy in the electric field, set by the voltage) and an inductor (energy in the magnetic field, set by the current — the current's “momentum”). On a spring they are the spring (stretch) and the mass (momentum). The momentum is what carries the system past its target. Heat has no momentum: there is no thermal inductor. Heat flow is driven only by the temperature difference, so as the gap closes, the flow dies away with it, and it reaches zero exactly at the target — nothing is left to carry T past Ts. Overshooting would mean heat continuing to flow from the colder side to the hotter side, which the second law of thermodynamics rules out for a passive system.

Damping — the cases, and which one we have. A system with two stores is second order, and its behaviour after a step is set by its damping ratio, ζ, in the standard form d2y/dt2 + 2ζωn dy/dt + ωn2 y = ωn2 ys:

  • Underdamped, ζ < 1 — complex roots: it overshoots and rings, the swings dying inside a decaying envelope. Only possible with inertia — an inductor, a mass, moving water — or with a feedback loop that has a delay or switches on and off, like a thermostat with lag.
  • Critically damped, ζ = 1 — a repeated real root: the fastest possible approach with no overshoot.
  • Overdamped, ζ > 1 — two real roots: a sum of two exponentials, slower and S-shaped at the start, no overshoot. Two heat stores joined by thermal resistances — two capacitors in an RC ladder — always land here: their roots are always real, however the values are chosen. That is the air-and-water system on the Water Level tab.
  • First order — no ζ at all. One store, one real root, a pure exponential: the fastest response at the start, closing on the target with no overshoot. That is the air-only system on this tab.
First order — this system (one store)Overdamped, ζ = 2.5 — two stores (air + water)Critically damped, ζ = 1Underdamped, ζ = 0.2 — needs inertia

Step responses, all closing on the same target (the dashed line at 1). The solid orange curve is this system: first order, a pure exponential. The blue dashed curve is what two heat stores give — overdamped, slower to start, still no overshoot. The thin grey dotted curve, ringing around the target, is what an underdamped system does; heat on its own cannot produce it, because it needs a second kind of store with momentum.

Heating and cooling. The exponential runs the same way in both directions because the model treats the pad as held at Ts by the thermostat — like a voltage source, which can take current back as well as supply it. A real heating pad can only add heat, so in a truly ideal, perfectly insulated container the air could warm up but never cool down: cooling needs a path for heat to leave, through slightly leaky glass. That leak is exactly the resistor Newton's law of cooling describes.

6. Expanding into Fourier — heat as a flow

Newton's law tracks one temperature in time. Fourier's analysis of heat goes further: heat is a flow through the air itself, driven by how temperature changes from place to place. That gives the heat equation:

∂T/∂t = α ∂2T/∂z2,   α = k / (ρcp)

7. Adding position — the distribution down the height

To get the distribution along the height — the second graph above — we add a second variable besides the temperature difference: position z, measured down from the panel. The temperature is now T(z, t), not one number, and its steady shape is the normalised profile θ(z) in the equations above.

Simplification 7 — Steady-state filter: transient → steady state only

We can ignore the transient and look at the steady state only — but only when it is applicable: only when a variable is held constant long enough for the transient to die away, t ≥ ts, where ts is the time it takes to reach steady state. Here that variable is temperature, held at the setpoint by the thermostat. Move the slider and the transient is real again until it settles. In the shorthand notation of section 11 this step appears as its own block: the steady-state filter — its symbol is defined on the Symbols tab.

Everything above follows heat in one direction, straight down — that is simplification 1 again: the heat is assumed to go straight down, the whole terrarium is heated this way, and changes in the other two directions are ignored. The real pad is inset from the terrarium walls, so heat also spreads sideways, and a full picture needs the actual 3D geometry. That is its own step, for later — it does not change the one-direction story, it completes it.

9. The filter model of temperature distributions

Distance acts as a filter. Reading the distribution itself as a filter is the filter model: distance acts as a filter for temperature. The temperature at the panel is the signal going in, at full strength. Travelling down through the air, it is progressively attenuated: near the top, close to the panel, most of it gets through; further down, less and less, and only the smoothed, weakened remainder reaches the bottom. The attenuation is the smooth exponential itself — it starts falling at the panel and keeps falling, steadily, all the way down.

That attenuation is a transform. A transform is an operation that takes a signal, or a response, in one form and gives it in another form. The transform of this system is attenuation over distance: the signal that goes in at the panel comes out weaker with every step of distance through the air, reshaped by that travel. In the block diagrams of section 11 that transform is carried by the exponential attenuation curve, α(x), sitting below the mixer and feeding up into it — because the transform is applied as a product: the main signal, Tair(t), is multiplied by α(x), which is why a transform here is also called a mixer.

So the temperature distribution is the filter model: the distribution down the height of the tank, in one direction only — straight down, read as a signal passing through a filter whose strength runs out with distance. The smooth exponential curves are the actual response.

The filter distribution again. The exponential curve — full at the panel, decaying smoothly all the way down. If the temperature changes, the whole distribution travels through a transient in time before it settles onto the new curve. For the simulator it is just as well to use the exponential model.

10. Towards system models — signals, filters, and the vocabulary

Why this matters. The point of modelling this one simple system carefully is that the goal is to make system models for other things later — ecological things included. On the heating-panel system we picked, we can now say we have the system modelled quite well using a filter: the panel puts a temperature signal in at the top, and distance down the tank attenuates it in exactly the way the filter model describes. That is an interesting parallel into electronics, and parallels like it are a real way in: the air warming toward the pad temperature is a capacitor charging through a resistor (section 5), and a filter here is the same shape of idea as a filter in electronics. The same ecological concept can often be reached through a mechanical parallel, an electrical parallel, or a thermodynamic one.

But the classification is our own. We do not want to classify system diagrams for ecology only by borrowing the parallel — always calling things by their electronics or mechanics name. So we keep the parallel as a parallel, and give the thing in front of us its own name. This filter is not, for our purposes, an electronic low-pass filter: it is a filter made of air, distance and heat, so we call it a thermodynamic filter. And the charging is not, for our purposes, just an RC circuit: what matters in the diagram is the kind of signal it produces — a smooth closing-in on a target, fast at first and slowing as it arrives — so we call it an exponential signal.

That gives us our first two classifications for system diagrams, and a first subclass. The definitions below are the vocabulary this note concludes with — the words we will use from here on.

Signal — a quantity that carries change through a system: it has a value that varies — with time, with position, or with both — and a system diagram is, in large part, a picture of what happens to its signals. The temperature at the panel, and the temperature at each height below it, is a signal. Signals are classified by the shape of that variation.

Filter — a part of a system that a signal passes through and is changed by: part of the signal gets through, part is attenuated or held back, so the signal that comes out is a reshaped version of the signal that went in. A filter is classified by what it lets through and what it cuts off.

Thermodynamic filter — our name, for system diagrams, for a filter whose filtering is done by a thermodynamic process rather than by an electronic component. This note’s example is the first one: distance through air acting on temperature — attenuating steadily with distance below the panel, the spatial profile an exponential (section 9). In our classification it is a thermodynamic filter.

Exponential signal — the first subclass of signal. An exponential signal closes in on a target at a rate proportional to the gap that remains — fast while the gap is big, slowing as it shrinks, settling with no overshoot. It is what any first-order system produces: one store filling or emptying through one resistance. The RC circuit is the electrical parallel, but the classification names the signal, not the parallel. The temperature signals in this note are exponentials — not as a simplification of something else, but because the physics is first order (section 5).

Oscillator — a different subclass, kept for contrast and for later: a signal that swings around a target — overshooting it, coming back, and, if it is damped, settling in swings that get smaller inside a decaying envelope. An oscillator needs a system with two kinds of store that trade energy — a mass and a spring, an inductor and a capacitor — or a feedback loop with delay. Temperature in this sealed tank has neither, so no oscillator appears in this note's diagrams. Other systems we model later — mechanical ones, or populations in ecology — may well produce them, and the diagram should say which kind it is dealing with.

How a filter and a signal interact, to produce a new signal. Left: the input is an exponential in time — the temperature at the panel side, closing on its new value. Middle: that signal travels down through the filter — the distribution with height — and at any chosen depth the filter passes only part of it. Right: what comes out at that depth is a new signal — the temperature at a point: the same exponential in time, scaled down by the filter at that depth. Temperature at a point = exponential (in time) × filter (in space), read at one depth. (Real heat also takes time to diffuse down, so deeper points lag a little; the product model of simplification 6 leaves that delay out.)

Linear signal — another kind of signal, for contrast: a signal whose variation is proportional and straight-line — double the input and you double the change, with no closing-in on a target and no cutoff. Not every signal is an exponential; some may be linear, and the diagram should say which kind it is dealing with.

The diagrams below are drawn in that light. Every signal in the chain is an exponential signal from the start — the pad signal TH(t) and the air signal Tair(t) — so no conversion step is needed: the exponential goes straight into distance — the thermodynamic filter, a transform / mixer (⊗), and what the filter outputs is the temperature over time at a position: T(x, t).

Transform — also called a mixer — an operation that takes a signal, or a response, in one form and gives it in another form. Calling it a mixer can be clearer: a mixer combines its inputs as a product. Filters can be classified as transforms — as mixers: a filter takes the signal that goes in and transforms / mixes it into the reshaped signal that comes out, so a filter is one kind of transform / mixer — the kind that does its transforming by passing part of the signal and attenuating the rest. In this case a transform is used for attenuation over distance: the signal that goes in at the panel comes out weaker with every step of distance through the air, reshaped by that travel — that weakening-over-distance is the transform this note works with.

Attenuation α(x) — the attenuation transform — the spatial part of the system, and not a symbol type of its own: it is an exponential signal used as a transform, mixed with the time signal by multiplication, and it is drawn with the same symbol as an exponential signal. It is the factor by which the temperature signal is weakened at a distance x from the panel. It is 1 at the panel — the signal at full strength — and falls steadily with distance, never quite reaching zero: an exponential decay over distance, α(x) = e−x/δ, where δ is the depth scale (how far the heat reaches before it has dropped to about 37%). It is the shape of the distribution graph at the top of this tab, normalised so the panel reads 1. So attenuation is not a new kind of signal: it is the exponential signal, running over distance instead of time — which is why the system diagram draws it with the exponential symbol, in its falling form, below the mixer. At the mixer it multiplies the time signal, Tair(t) × α(x) = Tair(t) at x: time and space kept as two separate factors, which is simplification 6.

Mixer (⊗) vs adder (⊕). These are two different operations on signals, and the diagrams keep them apart. A mixer combines its inputs as a product — one signal scales the other, as the attenuation α(x) scales the temperature signal at the mixer. An adder combines its inputs as a sum — the signals are added together into one, as where we add the transient state in with the output of the position distribution filter / transform / mixer, or where TH(x) and TH(t) add in the system diagram in section 11. Product gets the mixer, ⊗; addition gets the adder, ⊕.

Simplification 5 — Pad temperature constant in space: T(t, x, y) → TH(t)

Before the chain can start, the pad's temperature field T(t, x, y) is taken as constant in the spatial dimensions of the pad — the same everywhere on the pad — so the position parameters x and y are removed and what remains is TH(t), a function of time only. And TH(t) is itself an exponential signal: the pad does not jump to its new value, it closes in on it — the pad is a heat store too, charging through its own resistance. Space is not gone for good — it comes back later, in simplified form, as the attenuation α(x) at the mixer (simplification 6), with the heat travelling straight down only on the way (simplification 1).

Simplification 2 — TH = Ts at steady state

The heating pad temperature is set equal to the slider setting: TH = Ts, the steady-state constant value. Before that, the chain starts from two values: To, the previous steady-state value, and Ts, the new slider setting. Their difference, from the difference operator, is the signal that drives everything after it — TH starts at To and changes toward Ts based on that difference.

So, to fix the vocabulary: we have two classifications — signals and filters. Under signals, our first subclass is the exponential signal — and the temperature signal here is one, because the system is first order: one heat store, like one capacitor charging through a resistor. The oscillator is a second subclass, kept for systems that have inertia or delayed feedback; it does not appear here. And the heating-panel temperature distribution is classified here as a thermodynamic filter. Filters can also be classified as transforms — also called mixers, symbol ⊗: the attenuation transform, α(x), is mixed with the main signal at the mixer, as a product. Every block symbol used in the diagrams of section 11 — the difference operator and the addition operator (the adder, ⊕), the Boltzmann distribution, the medium change, the exponential signal (also used for the attenuation α(x)), the mixer (⊗), the steady-state filter, the constant signal, and the piecewise linear approximation — is defined on the Symbols tab, each symbol beside its definition.

Every time signal in it is an exponential signal, because every stage is first order — one heat store closing in on the stage before it. It shows the fuller pad signal, TH(x), the position part, adding with TH(t), the time part, at the adder, followed by another exponential signal block for TH(t) again before the medium change — because TH(x) is constant, the position part is the same everywhere, so even after the addition it just collapses to TH(t) again, and that exponential after the adder is still TH(t). The attenuation α(x) — the transform of this system, an exponential decay over distance — sits below the mixer and feeds up into it, multiplying the Tair(t) signal. The final output is Tair at x and t ≥ ts.

Read it left to right: To and Ts, the difference operator, the Boltzmann block, the exponential signal block for TH(t), then the adder, where TH(x) adds with the exponential TH(t), followed by another exponential signal block for TH(t) again before the medium change — because TH(x) is constant, the sum just collapses to TH(t) again. Then: medium change (pad → air) → exponential signal: Tair(t) → mixer, ⊗, with the attenuation α(x) below it — an exponential decay curve, the transform of this system — feeding up into it and multiplying → exponential signal after the mixer, Tair(t) at position x → steady-state filter (only t ≥ ts) → constant signal: Tair at x and t ≥ ts.

All of the block diagram symbols — the symbol on the left, its definition on the right — sorted into two groups: operators, which act on signals, and signals, the quantities that pass between them.

Operators

An operator takes one or more signals in and puts a signal out: it compares, adds, multiplies, carries a signal across a medium, filters it in time, or converts a transform into another form.

Difference operator. What it represents is a comparison between two steady values: the previous steady-state value, To, and the new slider setting, Ts. Their difference, Ts − To, is the gap the whole rest of the chain has to close — its size says how far the temperature has to travel, and its sign says which way: up if Ts > To, down if Ts < To. Nothing has heated or cooled yet at this step; the difference operator only measures the change that has been asked for.

Addition operator — the adder, ⊕. What it represents is two signals combined into one by adding them: the pair of the difference operator, for the places where a signal is genuinely the sum of two parts. Its place in this note is the system diagram in section 11, where TH(x) — the position part of the pad signal, a constant — adds with TH(t) — the time part, an exponential signal. Because TH(x) is constant, the output of the adding is an exponential that just becomes TH(t) again: adding a constant to the exponential does not change its kind. Adding is this operator; multiplying is the mixer, ⊗; subtracting is the difference operator.

Mixer — ⊗. What it represents is one signal scaling another by multiplication: a transform carried out as a product. In this note the mixer is where the temperature signal meets space: the exponential Tair(t) runs through the attenuation and is multiplied by it — Tair(t) × α(x) = Tair(t) at position x — so what comes out is the same signal, reduced by exactly the factor the distance x imposes. The mixer does not add anything of its own; it applies one input to the other. (Addition is a different operation — the adder, ⊕, defined beside the difference operator above.)

Medium change. What it represents is the signal crossing from one medium into another — here, heat passing from the solid heating pad into the air — and coming out as the corresponding signal in the new medium: TH(t) becomes Tair(t). The two sides are not the same substance and do not respond the same way: the pad is held at its temperature by the thermostat, while the air has to be brought there by contact. The medium-change math: the air chases the pad by Newton's law, dTair/dt = k·(TH − Tair), so Tair(t) = TH − (TH − To)·e−kt — an exponential in the air, closing in on the pad temperature, with the coupling k set by the contact between pad and air. In circuit terms it is the step from one capacitor to the next through a resistor. The kind of signal does not change across it: exponential in, exponential out — what changes is the medium carrying it, and with it the constants that govern it.

Steady-state filter. What it represents is keeping only the settled part of a signal and discarding its transient: a filter in time, not in space. It stands for the judgement that the variable has been held constant long enough — t ≥ ts, where ts is the time the settling takes — that the travel from the old value to the new one can be ignored and only the arrived value kept. It is a filter because it passes part of what reaches it and holds back the rest: the steady state passes, the transient does not. This is simplification 7 as a step in the diagram, and it applies only when that condition actually holds — move the slider and the transient is real again until it settles.

Piecewise linear approximation. What it represents is a transform converter for attenuation. A transform converter is a block in a system diagram that takes a transform in one form and puts it out in another form: it changes the form of the transform — the form of the response, not the kind of signal — the underlying transform, what it does to the signal, stays exactly the same; only the form it is carried in changes. That is what separates it from a signal converter: a signal converter would change the kind of signal itself, while a transform converter changes only the form a response is expressed in. A transform converter has no letter notation — it is named for the conversion it performs. This converter works on the attenuation over distance: in goes the attenuation as it actually is in space — an exponential decay, the signal weakening smoothly and steadily with distance from the panel — and out comes the same attenuation converted into a piecewise linear approximation: straight-line pieces standing in for the smooth exponential. The attenuation itself is unchanged — the same weakening, at the same distances — only the form it is carried in changes. This symbol is not used in the Temperature block diagram — the attenuation there is carried as the exponential itself, in the exponential curve below the mixer — but it is kept here because it can be used later.

Signals

A signal is a quantity that carries change through the system — what flows along the arrows between the operators. Each symbol shows the shape of that change.

Boltzmann distribution. What it represents is the spread of energies across all the particles of the air at one instant — few nearly still, most in the middle, a long tail of a few racing — and the temperature that spread amounts to. It stands for two steps: simplification 3, the whole set of particles and their bounces replaced by this one distribution, and simplification 4, that distribution collapsed by its integral average, ⟨E⟩ = ∫ E · P(E) dE = (3/2) kB T, into the single number T. It is made before the diagram even starts and carried through the whole chain — every temperature anywhere in the diagrams is already this average, not any one particle's motion.

Exponential signal. What it represents is a signal that closes on its target at a rate proportional to the gap that remains — fast while the gap is big, slowing as the gap shrinks, levelling off as it arrives: the Newton settling of section 5, T(t) = Ts − (Ts − To)·e−kt. It is the signal of any first-order system — one store filling through one resistance, like a capacitor charging in an RC circuit — and because a first-order system has no inertia, it never overshoots or swings. It is the true shape of every time signal in the Temperature diagram, not a simplification: TH(t) at the pad, before and after the adder, Tair(t) after the medium change, and Tair(t) at x after the mixer — attenuation scales an exponential's size without changing its kind, so the signal at position x is still this same exponential settling, only weaker. Whether it climbs or falls is set by the signal itself, by the sign of Ts − To: heating and cooling are the same exponential, closing the gap from opposite sides.

Also the attenuation α(x). The weakening of the temperature signal with distance from the panel is not a separate kind of block — it is this same exponential, running over distance instead of time: full strength at the panel, decaying steadily all the way down. So in the system diagram the attenuation below the mixer is drawn with this symbol, in its falling form. It is the transform of the system, multiplied into the main signal at the mixer, and it is where space comes back into the diagram in simplified form: simplification 6.

The equivalent diagrams. This symbol can be drawn four ways — the curve climbing or falling, toward the right or toward the left — and all four are equivalent symbols: each one stands for the same exponential signal, the same closing-on-the-target at a rate proportional to the gap. When the direction is specifically known, the drawing that matches it can be used: heating, Ts > To, climbs — drawn here up to the right, the form this note uses — and cooling, Ts < To, falls. But when the direction is unknown, the four are interchangeable: any one of them can be drawn, and it still means the exponential — the direction is then carried by the signal itself, by the sign of Ts − To, not by which way the curve in the circle happens to lean.

Constant signal. What it represents is a signal that no longer changes: one value, held. It is the end of the chain — the temperature at position x after the transient has been filtered away, T constant at steady state — the state the whole system was travelling toward from the moment the difference operator measured the gap. Every earlier step exists to produce this: a settled temperature, at a place, that stays where it is until the setting changes again.

With water — a second temperature, T_water

The water is a second medium with its own temperature, Twater. The pad still drives the air, and now the air drives the water across the surface:

Air:   dTair/dt = kair·(Theating pad − Tair) − ks·(Tair − Twater)

Water:   dTwater/dt = kw·(Tair − Twater)   (k_w is small — water has far more heat capacity, so T_water moves slowly and lags T_air)

Steady state:   Tair = Twater = Ts,   and total water is conserved:   H2O total = H2O liquid + H2O gaseous   (sealed tank — water only moves between liquid and vapour)

A terrarium with air and water in it, and a surface between them. This note is about that surface — how the balance between liquid and vapour shifts when the temperature changes, and how it closes in on the new equilibrium.

1. Three mediums now — pad, air, water

The tank now has water in it, filled up to the water level, with air above it. Heat now travels through three mediums in a chain: the heating pad hands heat to the air, and the air hands heat to the water across the surface. That is why the heat distribution had to be redone — the animation and the second graph above show one temperature profile running through all three, and the water part behaves differently: water has a much larger heat capacity, so it warms and cools slowly, and it evens the temperature out with depth instead of letting it fade quickly the way air does.

The tank is sealed and the water level is steady in the long run: H2O total = H2O liquid + H2O gaseous. Water is never created or lost — it only moves between the liquid and the vapour in the air. The point where the two balance is the saturation point, and that saturation point is the equilibrium point of this system.

2. The step from the simple solution to T_water

Start from the simple solution: one temperature for the air, and two named temperatures in the story — Ts, the setpoint, which is Theating pad, the pad temperature the thermostat holds. One equation, one exponential.

Adding water means adding one more temperature: Twater. Nothing about the pad or the air equation is thrown away — the air equation gains one extra term, the heat it loses to (or gains from) the water, and the water gets its own equation, driven by the gap (Tair − Twater). Because water's heat capacity is so much bigger, its constant kw is small: Twater chases Tair slowly. That lag is exactly what the live graph shows — move the slider and Tair moves first, Twater follows behind. In the steady state the chase ends: Tair = Twater = Ts. When the temperature changes, the system passes through a transient — water temporarily evaporates or condenses more — until it reaches the new equilibrium at the new saturation point.

3. The water surface and hydrogen bonds

The liquid itself is mostly still. Almost everything happens at the surface, where molecules constantly leave into the air (evaporation) and land back (condensation). To see why the surface behaves as it does, look at how water molecules connect.

Molecules at the surface, magnified. The dashed hydrogen bonds join the hydrogen of one molecule to the oxygen of its neighbour.

What a hydrogen bond is. Inside one molecule, the two hydrogens are strongly bonded to their own oxygen — those covalent bonds do not come apart. A hydrogen bond happens between molecules, and it is much weaker. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the oxygen end of a molecule is slightly negative and each hydrogen end slightly positive: every water molecule is a tiny dipole, a little magnet-like object with a positive and a negative end. The slightly positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen of its neighbour — that attraction is a hydrogen bond.

Hydrogen bonds are only about a twentieth as strong as the covalent bonds inside the molecule, so the thermal jiggling that is temperature breaks them constantly, and new ones form a moment later with whichever neighbour is close. Liquid water is a flickering network of little attractions that stretch, snap and reconnect. Those bonds are also why water holds together at all — cohesion: to leave the liquid, a molecule must break its hydrogen bonds, and that costs energy. At the surface there are no neighbours above, so a surface molecule's bonds pull it only sideways and down, back into the liquid — surface tension, a surface always being pulled inward, like a stretched skin. That is why we can ignore the mostly-still bulk and focus on the surface: it is the only unbalanced place, and the only place molecules leave or arrive.

4. The missing step — temperature → energy fluctuations at the surface

One step was missing between the temperature slider and the moving surface, and it is this conversion. A temperature is an average particle energy, so a temperature fluctuation is an energy fluctuation — at the surface, ΔE ≈ ms cp ΔT for the surface layer. That energy is what the surface spends and receives: a surface molecule can only leave the liquid if its share of the energy is enough to break its hydrogen bonds — the price L in the Clapeyron equation — and the share of molecules rich enough sits in the tail of the Boltzmann distribution, ∝ e−L/(kB T).

So when the temperature steps, the energy at the surface steps with it: warm the surface and the tail fattens, so evaporation wins until the air holds its new, larger saturated share; cool it and the tail thins, so condensation wins until the share falls back. How fast the net exchange runs is proportional to how far the vapour still is from saturation, so the split closes on its new value exponentially — and once it arrives, evaporation and condensation balance and the net flow stops. Each molecule's escape is random, but the net rate is an average over an enormous number of them, so the result is a smooth relaxation, not a swing.

One honest warning about size: converted into a distance, this is tiny. The vapour the air above the water can hold is only grams, spread over the whole surface, so the level itself moves by micrometres, not millimetres. Do not expect to see the level travel. That is why the H2O graph above and the live partition graph in section 7 track amounts of water, liquid and gas, rather than a distance.

5. Clapeyron — the equilibrium moves with temperature

The Clapeyron equation describes the equilibrium between the liquid and its vapour: at any temperature the vapour settles at one saturation pressure. Below it, more molecules leave than land — evaporation. Above it, more land than leave — condensation. Its Clausius–Clapeyron form (in the equations above) involves exactly the quantities you would expect: enthalpy (through L, the energy price of breaking the hydrogen bonds), entropy folded into the same balance, volume change, pressure and temperature. Its message: change the temperature and you move the equilibrium target — the saturation point itself shifts.

Now the journey to that target. The net exchange at the surface is driven by the gap between where the vapour is and where saturation says it should be — exactly the shape of Newton's law: rate ∝ (target − current). So the amount of vapour in the air is one more store filling through a resistance, an RC charge again, and it closes on its new target exponentially. Nothing in the exchange has momentum: once the air is saturated, the net rate is zero, so the vapour cannot carry on past the target.

6. Why the water does not ring — two stores, overdamped

It is tempting to picture the surface as a spring, pulled toward a new equilibrium and swinging past it. Physically, the heat and vapour exchange does not behave that way, and the reason is the same one as on the Temperature tab (section 5 of that note): ringing needs two kinds of store that hand energy back and forth — a mass and a spring, an inductor and a capacitor — so that momentum can carry the system past its target. Heat and vapour have no momentum.

Two heat stores are two capacitors. Adding water adds a second store, so the system is now second order — but both stores are the same kind: heat capacities, joined by thermal resistances (pad → air → water). In circuit terms that is an RC ladder, two capacitors and two resistors, and an RC ladder's roots are always real. That puts it in the overdamped case, ζ > 1, whatever the values: the response is a sum of two exponentials, a fast one (the air) and a slow one (the water). Twater lags behind Tair and starts off slowly in an S shape, but neither ever overshoots the setpoint. That is what the live graph at the top of this tab shows.

Particles pushing on the surface do not make it swing either. Vapour pressure presses on the whole surface equally, and any change in pressure evens out across the tank in milliseconds, at the speed of sound. Sloshing needs one side pushed harder than the other, so an even push from uniform heating gives only a tiny, slow, even change in level — no tilt and no ringing. The random impacts of individual molecules do ripple the surface — thermal capillary waves — but these are a fraction of a nanometre high and random: fluctuation, not oscillation.

Where real ringing would come from. A water surface can ring — but mechanically, not thermally. Tilt or bump the tank and the water sloshes: gravity is the restoring force (the spring), the moving water's mass is the inertia, and viscosity is the damping. For a tank this size the main slosh has a period of about a second and dies out over seconds to tens of seconds. Heating does not start it, and it does not make the temperature swing. The other real source is a controller: a thermostat that switches on and off, or a heater that keeps pushing after the target is reached, can make a temperature hunt around its setpoint. That comes from the feedback loop, not from the heat itself — this model's heater backs off smoothly in proportion to the gap, so it does not do that.

7. H₂O total = H₂O liquid + H₂O gas — the partition relaxes

Now follow the split of the water itself. The tank is sealed, so the total never changes — it only divides: H2O total = H2O liquid + H2O gas. Temperature sets the target split, through the saturation point: warm the tank and the equilibrium gas share grows while the liquid share falls by the same amount; cool it and the split moves back. But the split does not jump — net evaporation or condensation runs at a rate proportional to how far the split still is from its target, so the gas share closes on its new value exponentially, fast at first and slowing as it arrives, with no overshoot.

This is the complete model for the water level in a container, based on temperature alone. The whole chain, in order: temperature changes the saturation point → the saturation point is the equilibrium point → when the temperature changes, the equilibrium itself changes → the transient is simply the system travelling to the new equilibrium. Nothing else is needed: give the model a temperature and it gives you where the water must end up; give it a temperature change and the relaxation gives you the journey — an exponential closing-in on the new steady state. The water level is not a separate thing to solve afterwards; it is this partition, read at the surface.

It also ties back to the temperature distribution. The split is not set by some single tank temperature — it is set at the surface, by the temperature the distribution graph (second graph at the top of this tab) has at the water-level depth. That surface temperature sets the local saturation point there: how much vapour the air right above the surface can hold. Read the distribution to get the surface temperature, the surface temperature gives the saturation point, the saturation point gives the target split — and the two overlaid curves, here and in the graph at the top of this tab, then let you read off, at any given time, how much water is in the air as gas and how much is in the liquid, mid-transient or settled, with the dashed total confirming the sum never changes.

8. Temperature drives, the amounts accumulate — driver and integral

The two live graphs on this tab are not two separate stories — one is the cause, the other is the accumulated effect. The temperature graph is the driver. The liquid/gas graph is its integral.

Temperature is energy — the average energy of the particles — and a temperature difference is a rate waiting to happen. Everywhere in this model, a flow is proportional to a gap: heat flow ∝ (Ts − T), and dT/dt = k·(gap). So read the temperature graph as a graph of driving force over time: the vertical distance between Tair and Twater at any moment is the push acting at that moment. A wide gap means change is being driven hard; a narrow gap means it is barely being driven at all. That is also why the auto-zoom on that graph matters — as the two curves converge the push fades, and the zoom keeps the small remaining push visible instead of letting it flatten into the axis.

The push acts at the surface, and the surface is where the two graphs meet. The water temperature at the surface sets the saturation point there — through Clapeyron, a warmer surface has a higher saturation pressure — and the saturation point sets how much water the air holds at equilibrium: the target gas share on the H2O graph. The air temperature controls the supply side: it decides how fast energy arrives at the surface, to pay the hydrogen-bond price L for molecules leaving, or how fast energy is carried away when molecules land back and give that price up. Twater names the target; Tair names how fast energy is being delivered toward it.

H2O gas(t) = H2O gas(0) + ∫0t (evaporation − condensation) dt,   with   (evaporation − condensation) ∝ the driving gap

The amounts do not respond to the temperature itself — they respond to the net exchange rate, summed over time. While Tair and Twater differ, the gap is not zero, evaporation and condensation do not balance, and the integral keeps accumulating: every second of gap adds a little more water to the air, or takes a little back, so the liquid and gas curves keep moving. The total moved is the area under the gap curve, not just its height — a short, wide gap and a long, narrow gap can accumulate exactly the same change.

When the temperatures converge — Tair = Twater = Ts, the two curves on the zoomed graph closing onto each other — the gap goes to zero, evaporation balances condensation, and the integrand switches off. The integral stops accumulating, so the partition settles where it is: at the split belonging to that temperature, the one the equilibrium graph at the top of this tab reads off. Change the temperature again and the whole sequence restarts: new surface temperature, new saturation point, new target split — and a fresh gap on the temperature graph driving a fresh stretch of accumulation on the amounts graph.

So read the two graphs in that order. Temperature graph first: how big is the gap, which way does it point, how fast is it closing — the driver, in degrees, right now. Liquid/gas graph second: how much that driver has added up to so far — the integral, in percent, over the whole history. The driver can be back near zero while the amounts are still far from where they started, because the integral remembers everything the gap has done.

9. Signals, filters, and simplifications — the vocabulary in this system

The same two classifications used for system diagrams apply here: signals and filters. The definitions below are the vocabulary this note concludes with — the same words, carried into this system.

Signal — a quantity that carries change through a system: it has a value that varies — with time, with position, or with both — and a system diagram is, in large part, a picture of what happens to its signals. In this system the signals are Tair, Twater, the gap of the vapour from saturation, and the two amounts, H2O liquid and H2O gas. Signals are classified by the shape of that variation.

Filter — a part of a system that a signal passes through and is changed by: part of the signal gets through, part is attenuated or held back, so the signal that comes out is a reshaped version of the signal that went in. A filter is classified by what it lets through and what it cuts off, and by where its cutoff point falls.

Thermodynamic filter — our name, for system diagrams, for a filter whose filtering is done by a thermodynamic process rather than by an electronic component. In this system it is distance through the air, and then through the water, acting on temperature: the temperature signal leaves the panel at full strength, passes nearly whole near the top, breaks at the cutoff point, and is attenuated with distance below it — through the surface and down through the water as well as through the air above it. In electronics language it is modelled well as a low-pass filter; in our classification it is a thermodynamic filter.

Exponential signal — the first subclass of signal: a signal that closes in on its target at a rate proportional to the gap that remains, settling with no overshoot. It is what a first-order system produces — one store filling through one resistance, the RC charge — and it is the kind of signal in this system. Tair closes on the pad, the vapour share closes on saturation, and each is first order on its own. Twater is the second store in the chain, so it is a sum of exponentials — still no swing, because two heat stores joined by resistances are always overdamped (section 6).

Oscillator — a different subclass, for contrast: a signal that swings around a target — overshooting it, coming back, and settling in swings that get smaller inside a decaying envelope. It needs two kinds of store that trade energy, with momentum to carry the system past its target, or a feedback loop with delay. None of this system's temperatures or amounts is one. The water surface sloshing after a bump would be one — gravity as the spring, the moving water as the mass — but that is a mechanical signal, started by a disturbance, not by the temperature.

Constant — the settled limit with the travel removed. A signal drawn as constant — flat at its steady-state value, the target line at Ts, or the liquid/gas split drawn at its equilibrium value for each temperature in the H2O graph at the top of this tab — is a simplification. It shows only where the exponential ends up, and removes the travel: the closing-in by which the temperatures and the partition actually get there. The flat dashed H2O total line is exact for a sealed tank — the total really does not change — but it must not be read as the components being constant: H2O liquid and H2O gas underneath it change, mirroring each other as they close on their target split while their sum stays still.

Linear — the local push, not the journey. Where a relation here is written as linear — a flow proportional to a gap, dT/dt = k·(gap) — that straight-line law is exact for the rate at each moment, but the signal it produces over time is not a straight line: as the gap shrinks, so does the push, and the result is the exponential.

Linear signal — for contrast, a signal whose variation really is proportional and straight-line — double the input and you double the change, with no closing-in on a target and no cutoff. That is a different underlying kind, and the diagram should say so when it is dealing with one. The signals in this system are not that kind.

So, to fix the vocabulary here as well: we have two classifications — signals and filters. Under signals, our first subclass is the exponential signal, and the main signals of this system — the two temperatures and the liquid/gas split — are exponentials or sums of exponentials, because every store here is a heat or vapour store with no momentum. The oscillator stays in the vocabulary for systems with inertia or delayed feedback. The temperature distribution through the air and the water, modelled well as a low-pass filter, is classified here as a thermodynamic filter. And a signal shown as constant keeps only its settled value, leaving the travel out.
