This is the simplest terrarium model. A heating panel on top, and one variable: temperature. The terrarium is empty and sealed, with just air inside — and the job is to model how the heat distributes through that air.

The simplifications, in order, before we begin. This model rests on nine simplifications, listed here in the order the shorthand block diagram in section 11 uses them — and each one is mentioned again where it happens in the note below. 1 — a set of particles becomes a Boltzmann distribution (made before the diagram even starts, and carried through the whole chain). 2 — that distribution becomes a single average temperature, by the integral average. 3 — the pad temperature is taken as constant in space: T(t, x, y) becomes TH(t), the position parameters x and y removed, because the pad is assumed the same temperature everywhere. 4 — the pad temperature is set equal to the slider setting at steady state: TH = Ts. 5 — the oscillator signal is converted to an exponential: the envelope signal converter — the envelope operator — keeps the envelope and drops the swing. 6 — the heat is assumed to go straight down, and the whole terrarium is taken to be heated this way: changes in the other two directions are ignored, so the temperature varies only with depth down the tank. 7 — space is brought back in a simplified form only: attenuation α(x), instead of the full spatial field that simplification 3 removed. 8 — the Bode plot: the filter is also drawn in Bode form — flat, then falling in a straight line — but that shape belongs to the frequency response (how much of an oscillation gets through, against frequency, at a fixed depth), not to the spatial curve: in space the attenuation stays the exponential, one straight line on the log-θ graph, with no flat section and no corner. 9 — the transient is ignored and only the steady state is kept (the steady-state filter), but only when that is applicable — when a variable, here temperature, is held constant long enough, t ≥ ts.

1. How the heating pad works

The pad converts electrical energy into heat: current pushed through a resistive element (Joule heating) warms the panel, and the thermostat holds its surface at the setpoint temperature, Theating pad — the temperature you set with the slider, also written Ts in the simple solution. We assume the heat is spread uniformly over the panel surface, and from that surface it radiates and conducts downward into the air. For now we follow the heat in one direction only — straight down. The pad is inset from the edges of the terrarium, so a full picture would need heating in three dimensions; that is left for later (section 8).

Simplification 6 — Heat goes straight down only

We assume the heat goes straight down, and that the whole terrarium just gets heated this way. Changes in the other two directions are ignored: sideways spread does not enter the model at all, so as the heat travels, the temperature varies only with depth down the tank — one direction, not three.

2. Particles first — temperature is motion

The air is a huge number of tiny particles, all moving and bouncing — off the glass, off the panel, off each other. Temperature is average particle motion: hotter air just means its particles are, on average, moving faster.

(1/2)m⟨v2⟩ = (3/2) kB T

When a slower air particle bounces off the hot panel surface, it tends to leave faster than it arrived — the bounce hands it energy. Collisions then share that extra motion through the tank. Heat entering the tank is this: faster bounces at the top, spreading downward.

Particles in the terrarium — nothing but air. Every dot is one air particle, coloured by its speed (blue = slow, orange/red = fast). Particles bounce off the glass and off the heating panel on top; a bounce off the hot panel hands a slow particle energy, so it leaves faster than it arrived. Move the temperature slider at the top of the tab and the whole swarm speeds up or slows down.

3. The Boltzmann distribution

Not every particle moves at the average speed. At any instant some are nearly still, most are in the middle, a few are racing. Temperature fixes the shape of the spread of speeds — the Boltzmann distribution. The chance of finding a particle with energy E falls off exponentially:

P(E) ∝ e−E / (kB T)

Raise T and the spread shifts to higher speeds and broadens — the fast tail grows.

Speeds — Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution probability density vs particle speed

Energies — Boltzmann distribution probability density vs particle energy

The Boltzmann distribution at each setpoint — same colours as the distribution graphs at the top of the tab, current setpoint bold. Left: how particle speeds are spread out. Few particles are near still, most sit around the peak, and a long fast tail runs off to the right; the dashed line marks the average speed, ⟨v⟩. Right: the same spread by energy — the chance of a particle carrying energy E falls off as e−E/(kB T), and the dashed line marks the average energy, (3/2)kB T. Raise T and both curves shift right, broaden and flatten, and the fast / high-energy tail grows — that tail is the part of the swarm in the particle diagram above drawn orange and red.

What “the average” means — it is an integral. When we say we take the average of the Boltzmann distribution, we do not read a single value off the curve. We add up every energy a particle could have, each one weighted by how likely the distribution says it is, over the whole curve — and that weighted sum is an integral. The brackets ⟨ ⟩ in this note mean exactly that integral average:

⟨E⟩ = ∫0∞ E · P(E) dE = (3/2) kB T

The distribution is normalised — its weights add to one, ∫ P(E) dE = 1 — so ⟨E⟩ is the balance point of the whole curve: every part of it counts, in proportion to its weight. The same integral over the speed curve gives ⟨v2⟩, and it is that, through (1/2)m⟨v2⟩ = ⟨E⟩ in section 2, that sets T. So yes: the average energy is got by integrating, and it is the energy of that average — the mean energy itself — that we then use. The whole spread goes in; one number, ⟨E⟩, comes out; and that number is the temperature.

Not the peak, not the midpoint, not the maximum. The peak of the speed curve is only the most probable speed — the single speed with the most particles near it — and it is not the average: on the left-hand graph the dashed ⟨v⟩ line sits to the right of the peak, because the long fast tail pulls the integral average up even though few particles live out there. There is no midpoint to take instead: the energy curve starts at 0 and tails off without ever quite ending, so it has no middle value — and no maximum either; the tail just gets thinner and thinner. And because energy goes as v2, that tail counts for more than its particle count suggests. One more consequence of averaging by integral: what we average is the energies, so ⟨E⟩ = (1/2)m⟨v2⟩ — which is not the energy of the average speed, (1/2)m⟨v⟩2. That comes out smaller, because averaging first and squaring afterwards throws away exactly the spread the integral keeps. Average the energies first — then the average energy is the temperature. The peak and the maximum never enter it.

And not the amplitude of the oscillator. These are two different averagings, at two different steps, and they are easy to mix up. The Boltzmann average is taken across particles, at one instant: thousands of different energies collapse, by the integral, into the one number T. The oscillator comes afterwards: that one number then varies in time, swinging around its setpoint while the tank settles, as the live graph at the top of this tab shows. The integral average is the centre line the oscillator swings around — its mean — not the size of its swing. The amplitude is a separate quantity: how far T departs from that centre before settling. Reading a point of the Boltzmann curve — its midpoint, or any other single point — off as an amplitude would mix the two steps together: the distribution is a spread over particles, the amplitude is a swing over time.

The Fourier comparison. The comparison to Fourier is close, and it is worth saying exactly where it holds. A Boltzmann distribution and a frequency spectrum are the same kind of object: a curve that says how much weight sits at each value of something — here, how many particles at each energy; in Fourier's picture, how much of a signal at each frequency. And energy and frequency are genuinely linked — for a wave, E grows in proportion to f, and for light the link is exact, E = hf — so an energy distribution can fairly be read as a kind of spectrum. To rebuild a time-domain signal from a spectrum you use all of it: every frequency component added back together, each with its weight — again an integral, over frequencies instead of energies. Simplification 2 below does the opposite job with the same tool: instead of keeping the whole spectrum in order to rebuild something from it, it uses the integral to collapse the whole distribution down to its one weighted average, ⟨E⟩, and throws the shape of the spread away. One number survives — the average energy, not the maximum, not the peak — and that number is what we call the temperature and feed to everything that follows.

Simplification 1 — Particles → Boltzmann distribution

Instead of tracking every particle and every bounce, we replace the whole set of particles with one Boltzmann distribution: a smooth curve for how the speeds are spread out.

Simplification 2 — Boltzmann distribution → Average temperature

From that whole spread we then take just the average — the average kinetic energy — and that average is the temperature: one number, T, standing in for the entire distribution. And “average” here means the integral average worked out above, ⟨E⟩ = ∫ E · P(E) dE — the whole curve collapsed to its weighted mean energy — not its peak, not a midpoint read off it, and not an oscillator amplitude.

4. Newton's law of cooling

Now the exchange between the pad and the air. The intuition behind Newton's law of cooling is that the rate of heat exchange is proportional to the temperature difference — the bigger the gap between pad and air, the faster heat flows — times a constant that bundles up the contact area, the air's mass and its heat capacity:

rate of heat exchange ∝ (Theating pad − T)

5. The differential equation, solved

Written as a differential equation for the air temperature T, with Ts the setpoint (the pad temperature):

dT/dt = k·(Ts − T)

Solved, it gives the transient you see in the live graph above:

T(t) = Ts − (Ts − T0)·e−kt

Fast change while the gap is big, slowing as the air closes in on the pad temperature, settling exponentially. This holds in both directions — heating up and cooling down are the same gap closing, from opposite sides.

Simplification 5 — Oscillator → exponential (the envelope signal converter — the envelope operator) · the temperature signal is an oscillator signal — the exponential is its simplification

The temperature signal here is an oscillator signal — the same vocabulary as the Water Level system. The live graph at the top of this tab now shows it in full: the solid orange curve is the actual signal, swinging around the setpoint inside its dashed orange envelope as it settles. The smooth exponential solved above — drawn on that graph as the white dashed line, behind everything — is its mean, and it is a simplification: exponential = the oscillator's envelope / mean with the swing removed. Keep the envelope and the mean and drop the swing and you get the exponential; the underlying signal is still the oscillator.

6. Expanding into Fourier — heat as a flow

Newton's law tracks one temperature in time. Fourier's analysis of heat goes further: heat is a flow through the air itself, driven by how temperature changes from place to place. That gives the heat equation:

∂T/∂t = α ∂2T/∂z2,   α = k / (ρcp)

7. Adding position — the distribution down the height

To get the distribution along the height — the second graph above — we add a second variable besides the temperature difference: position z, measured down from the panel. The temperature is now T(z, t), not one number, and its steady shape is the normalised profile θ(z) in the equations above.

Simplification 9 — Steady-state filter: transient → steady state only

We can ignore the transient and look at the steady state only — but only when it is applicable: only when a variable is held constant long enough for the transient to die away, t ≥ ts, where ts is the time it takes to reach steady state. Here that variable is temperature, held at the setpoint by the thermostat. Move the slider and the transient is real again until it settles. In the shorthand notation of sections 10–11 this step is drawn as its own block: the steady-state filter, a circle with an hourglass in it.

8. The real geometry in 3D — for later

Everything above follows heat in one direction, straight down — that is simplification 6 again: the heat is assumed to go straight down, the whole terrarium is heated this way, and changes in the other two directions are ignored. The real pad is inset from the terrarium walls, so heat also spreads sideways, and a full picture needs the actual 3D geometry. That is its own step, for later — it does not change the one-direction story, it completes it.

9. The filter model of temperature distributions

What a Bode plot is. A Bode plot is a way of drawing how a system responds to oscillations: how much of a signal gets through — its magnitude, in dB on the vertical axis — drawn against frequency, on a log scale, on the horizontal axis. It is the standard plot for filters. Its useful trick is the straight-line approximation — instead of drawing the smooth curve exactly, you approximate it as two straight lines: a horizontal line where the signal passes, then, at one corner point, a straight downward line where it is cut off and falls away. That corner is the cutoff point. Both halves of that definition matter for what follows: the flat-then-down shape, and the fact that the horizontal axis is frequency.

Why the Bode lines do not belong on this graph. Start with the temperature distribution we already have — normalised temperature θ against height, from the panel at the top down to the bottom of the tank. That distribution decays exponentially with height. Redraw it with height along the bottom on a plain linear scale and θ on a log scale — the graph titled The Filter Model of Temperature Distributions above. On that graph the exponential does exactly one thing: it straightens into one straight line. There is no horizontal segment and no corner in it — the spatial attenuation α(x) is a single exponential, falling from the panel all the way down. So the flat-then-down Bode overlay that used to be drawn over these curves has been removed: it was dressing the spatial curve up as something it is not. Height is not frequency — this graph is a spatial profile, not a frequency response.

The log scaling — why it is used, and what it does to the curve. On a linear scale the exponential distribution bunches up: most of the change happens near the top, the rest is squashed into the bottom, and H/2 does not sit at the halfway mark — it falls down near θ ≈ 0.29. The log scale is used because of one fact: the log of an exponential is a straight line. Put θ on a log scale and the exponential curve is stretched out and straightened — equal ratios take equal space instead of equal differences, so the bunched-up low end opens out, height reads evenly again on its linear scale, and H/2 sits back at its proper halfway place.

Would log-scaling the horizontal axis fix the mismatch? No. It is natural to ask whether the Bode shape failed to match because the horizontal axis was not log-scaled — but a Bode plot's horizontal axis is log frequency and its vertical axis is magnitude in dB. Our mismatch is the variable, not just the scaling: the horizontal axis here is distance, not frequency. Log-scaling the depth would not straighten anything — the exponential is taken of the depth itself, not of the log of the depth, so against log depth the line would bend more, not less. No rescaling of a distance axis turns a spatial profile into a frequency response.

Distance acts as a filter. Reading the distribution itself as a filter is still the filter model: distance acts as a filter for temperature. The temperature at the panel is the signal going in, at full strength. Travelling down through the air, it is progressively attenuated: near the top, close to the panel, most of it gets through; further down, less and less, and only the smoothed, weakened remainder reaches the bottom. But the attenuation is the smooth exponential itself — it starts falling at the panel and keeps falling, with no flat pass region and no cutoff point on the spatial curve.

Is it backwards? — no; it was drawn on the wrong graph. The Bode version sat flat at full heat first and only started falling after a cutoff point, while the exponential falls steadily from the top — and no, the lines were not simply backwards, and log-scaling the horizontal axis would not have fixed them, for the reason just given. The flat-then-down shape is a real shape — it just belongs somewhere else: to the frequency response. Let the surface temperature oscillate at frequency ω. At any fixed depth, the swing's amplitude is attenuated by e−x/δ, where the thermal skin depth δ = √(2α/ω) gets smaller as the oscillation gets faster — so slow oscillations pass down to that depth and fast ones die out before reaching it. Amplitude against frequency, at a fixed depth, really does run flat and then fall: that is a genuine low-pass Bode plot, and that is where the Bode Plot Converter of sections 10–11 applies. On the spatial graph, the exponential is the accurate version — and on the log-θ graph it is one straight line.

So the temperature distribution is the filter model: the distribution down the height of the tank, in one direction only — straight down, read as a signal passing through a filter whose strength runs out with distance. The smooth exponential curves are the actual response; on the log-θ graph above, each one is a single straight line.

The filter distribution again, with its versions side by side. Simplest version: the top half of the tank sits at full heat (θ = 1) down to H/2, then a linear filter falls in a straight line from H/2 to the bottom. The exponential curve is the more accurate version of the same distribution — full at the panel, decaying smoothly all the way down. No Bode lines are drawn over it: the flat-then-down Bode shape belongs to the frequency response, not to distance, as the paragraphs above set out. If the temperature changes, the whole distribution travels through a transient in time before it settles onto the new curve. For a simple distribution we can simplify to the linear steady state — but for the simulator it is just as well to use the exponential model, to be more accurate.

Simplification 8 — The Bode plot

The Bode plot is itself a simplification — and it belongs to the frequency response, not to this spatial curve. In space, the filter of distance has an exponential response: one straight line on the log-θ graph, no flat section, no corner. The Bode form — flat, then falling in a straight line — is the simple symbolic form for how the filter treats oscillations of different frequencies at a fixed depth (the work of the Bode Plot Converter, a transform converter, in sections 10–11). It is not the shape of the temperature distribution, and it is no longer drawn over it.

10. Towards system models — signals, filters, and the vocabulary

Why this matters. The point of modelling this one simple system carefully is that the goal is to make system models for other things later — ecological things included. On the heating-panel system we picked, we can now say we have the system modelled quite well using a low-pass filter: the panel puts a temperature signal in at the top, and distance down the tank attenuates it in exactly the way the filter model describes. That is an interesting parallel into electronics, and parallels like it are a real way in: the spring we used elsewhere is a mechanical model, a spring's charging and settling can also be compared to a capacitor charging in a circuit, and a filter here is the same shape of idea as a filter in electronics. The same ecological concept can often be reached through a mechanical parallel, an electrical parallel, or a thermodynamic one.

But the classification is our own. We do not want to classify system diagrams for ecology only by borrowing the parallel — always calling things by their electronics or mechanics name. So we keep the parallel as a parallel, and give the thing in front of us its own name. This filter is not, for our purposes, an electronic low-pass filter: it is a filter made of air, distance and heat, so we call it a thermodynamic filter. And the spring is not, for our purposes, just a mechanical spring: what matters in the diagram is the kind of signal it produces — a swing around a target that settles — so we call it an oscillator.

That gives us our first two classifications for system diagrams, and a first subclass. The definitions below are the vocabulary this note concludes with — the words we will use from here on.

Signal — a quantity that carries change through a system: it has a value that varies — with time, with position, or with both — and a system diagram is, in large part, a picture of what happens to its signals. The temperature at the panel, and the temperature at each height below it, is a signal. Signals are classified by the shape of that variation.

Filter — a part of a system that a signal passes through and is changed by: part of the signal gets through, part is attenuated or held back, so the signal that comes out is a reshaped version of the signal that went in. A filter is classified by what it lets through and what it cuts off, and by where its cutoff point falls.

Thermodynamic filter — our name, for system diagrams, for a filter whose filtering is done by a thermodynamic process rather than by an electronic component. This note's example is the first one: distance through air acting on temperature — attenuating steadily with distance below the panel, the spatial profile an exponential with no flat section and no corner (section 9). In electronics language its frequency response is modelled well as a low-pass filter; in our classification it is a thermodynamic filter.

Oscillator — the first subclass of signal. An oscillator is a signal that swings around a target or equilibrium value — overshooting it, coming back, and, if it is damped, settling onto it in swings that get smaller. The spring model produces an oscillator: a mechanical spring is the parallel that got us there, and a capacitor charging and discharging is an electrical parallel for the same kind of settling behaviour, but the classification names the signal, not the parallel. Many of the signals we will deal with will be oscillators.

What an oscillator is. The solid curve is the signal in time: it swings above and below its target, each swing smaller than the last, caged by the dashed envelope, until it settles on the target. The dashed line through the middle is its mean — keep the mean and the envelope and drop the swing, and the oscillator simplifies to the exponential.

How filters and oscillators interact, to produce a new signal. Left: the input is an oscillator in time — the temperature at the panel, swinging as it settles. Middle: that signal travels down through the filter — the distribution with height — and at any chosen depth the filter passes only part of it. Right: what comes out at that depth is a new signal — the temperature at a point: still an oscillator in time, but smaller (attenuated by the filter at that depth) and later (the swing takes time to travel down). Temperature at a point = oscillator (in time) × filter (in space), read at one depth.

The temperature signal in this note is an oscillator. That applies here, on the air-only system, just as it does in the Water Level system: the actual temperature swings around the setpoint and settles, as the live graph at the top of this tab shows in solid orange inside its dashed orange envelope. Where this note draws that signal as a smooth exponential — the white dashed mean on the live graph, and the solution T(t) = Ts − (Ts − T0) e−kt in section 5 — that exponential is a simplification of the oscillator: it is the oscillator's envelope / mean with the swing removed, not a different kind of signal.

Linear signal — another kind of signal, for contrast: a signal whose variation is proportional and straight-line — double the input and you double the change, with no swing around a target and no cutoff. Not every signal is an oscillator; some may be linear, and the diagram should say which kind it is dealing with.

Envelope signal converter — also called the envelope operator — a type of signal converter: a block in a system diagram that takes a signal in one form and puts it out in a simpler form: it changes the kind of signal, not where the signal is going. Its symbol is the curved dashed diamond defined below — a diamond whose diagonal sides are not straight lines but curved, each side an exponential curve like an envelope line, and concave inwards — every side caves in toward the centre of the diamond, never bowing out. Our first envelope signal converter is the step this note has been using all along without drawing it: oscillator signal in, exponential signal out. It keeps the oscillator's envelope and mean and drops the swing — the simplifications above, done as one step and drawn as one block. The conversion is something we do to the signal, so the rest of the diagram can work with a simple signal; it is not a process happening in the tank.

That ordering matters, and the diagrams below are drawn in that order. In a block diagram we will know that the true signal is an oscillator, and that the shape drawn for it is not the true shape — the simplification process is exactly what turns the oscillator into the simple signal. So the chain is drawn honestly: the oscillator goes into the envelope signal converter (the envelope operator) first, the converter outputs the exponential signal, and distance — the thermodynamic filter, a transform / mixer (⊗) — acts on that simplified signal, not on the raw oscillator. What the filter outputs is the temperature over time at a position: T(x, t).

Transform — also called a mixer — an operation that takes a signal, or a response, in one form and gives it in another form. Calling it a mixer can be clearer: a mixer combines its inputs as a product. Filters can be classified as transforms — as mixers: a filter takes the signal that goes in and transforms / mixes it into the reshaped signal that comes out, so a filter is one kind of transform / mixer — the kind that does its transforming by passing part of the signal and attenuating the rest. In this case a transform is used for attenuation over distance. For transform / mixer processes we use the mixer symbol: a circle with a × in it — ⊗.

Transform converter — a block in a system diagram that takes a transform in one form and puts it out in another form: it changes the form of the transform — the form of the response, not the kind of signal. That is what separates it from the envelope signal converter (the envelope operator): an envelope signal converter changes the kind of signal (oscillator in, exponential signal out), while a transform converter changes the form a response is drawn in. A transform converter has no letter notation — it is named, and drawn by its own symbol.

Bode Plot Converter — a type of transform converter, and our first one. It takes an exponential and makes it a linear piecewise function: the exponential filter of distance — the filter's normal response — goes in, and its linear Bode response comes out. The filtering itself does not change; only the form it is drawn in changes, from the exponential to the Bode plot — and that is what gives us a simple filter model. The flat-then-down Bode shape it produces legitimately belongs to the frequency response: the amplitude of an oscillation against its frequency, at a fixed depth — where the thermal skin depth δ = √(2α/ω) shrinks as the frequency rises, so slow oscillations pass and fast ones are attenuated. On the spatial distribution itself the exponential stays an exponential — one straight line on the log-θ graph — and the converter's Bode form is not drawn over it (section 9). Its symbol is the broken line defined in the symbol list below: a horizontal line from the left to the centre, at one third down from the top, then a break at the centre — no line joining the two halves — then a second horizontal line from the centre to the right, at one third up from the bottom, like a broken square wave.

Mixer symbol (⊗) vs adder symbol (⊕). For transform / mixer processes we use the mixer symbol — a circle with a × in it, ⊗ — a product. Where signals are added instead — like where we add the transient state in with the output of the position distribution filter / transform / mixer — we use the circle with a + in it before it — ⊕. Product gets ⊗; addition gets ⊕.

Shorthand block symbols — the notation. The definitions above name the parts; the shorthand notation draws them. Every block in the shorthand is a circle with a small graph or mark inside it that says what the block is or what kind of signal it carries, so the diagram can be read at a glance — the equations and the processes visible in the shapes themselves. The symbols are defined here, beside their definitions, before the diagram in section 11 uses them: each one is a block of the graph version above, simplified — its little graph shrunk into a circle — and that step, from the graphs to the circles, is the shorthand. Where an older name and a shorthand symbol cover the same thing, the shorthand name is the one used from here on.

Difference operator — circle with a minus sign. Two values go in, their difference comes out. The shorthand chain starts here: To, the previous steady-state value, and Ts, the slider setting, stacked on the left, both feed this circle, and it produces the difference signal, Ts − To — the gap the rest of the chain has to close.

Addition operator — the adder, ⊕ — circle with a plus sign. The pair of the difference operator, for when we need to add signals: two signals go in, their sum comes out. Wherever signals are added in a diagram this circle is used — for example where TH(x) adds in with TH(t) in the complex shorthand diagram in section 11, to give TH(x, t). Adding gets ⊕ (a +); multiplying gets the mixer ⊗ (a ×); subtracting gets this section's difference operator (a −).

Oscillator signal — circle with a sine wave. One cycle of a sine wave drawn about the horizontal middle of the circle — like a yin-yang dividing line, but not coloured in black or white. This is the mark of an oscillator signal wherever it appears in the chain: TH(t) at the pad is one, and after the medium change Tair(t) is another.

Boltzmann distribution — circle with a distribution curve. A peak with a long tail, the dashed line at the average ⟨E⟩. This is simplification 1 — many particles replaced by one distribution — together with simplification 2, the integral average of that distribution, ⟨E⟩ = ∫ E · P(E) dE = (3/2) kB T. It is the first simplification: a choice made before the diagram even starts, near the input, and carried through the whole chain — every temperature in the diagram is already this average. In the chain it is drawn near the input, where the average temperature is formed, before the medium change.

Medium change — circle with a hash. Three diagonal lines one way, then three diagonal lines perpendicular to them, crossing inside the circle — centred on the circle: the middle line of each set passes through the centre, and the other two sit evenly either side of it. The signal crosses from one medium into another and comes out as the corresponding signal in the new medium: here TH(t) becomes Tair(t). The medium-change math: the air chases the pad by Newton's law, dTair/dt = k·(TH − Tair), so Tair(t) = TH − (TH − To)·e−kt as its mean — an oscillator in the air, swinging around the pad temperature, with the coupling k set by the contact between pad and air. The kind of signal does not change across the block: oscillator in, oscillator out.

Envelope signal converter — the envelope operator — circle with a curved dashed diamond. A diamond in a circle, but the diamond is not made of straight diagonal lines: each diagonal side is curved like an exponential curve, like an envelope line — concave inwards, and dashed: the sides cave in toward the centre of the diamond, strongly, so the concavity is unmistakable — the top-left side rises up to the right, the top-right side comes down to the left, the bottom-left side descends down to the right, and the bottom-right side ascends up to the right — and the four curved dashed sides meet at the top, right, bottom and left points to form the diamond. That is what this symbol means: an envelope signal converter, also called the envelope operator — a type of signal converter. It takes the envelope of an oscillator signal and puts out an exponential. This is simplification 5, drawn as a block: oscillator in, exponential out, the swing dropped.

Exponential signal — circle with an exponential curve. The curve starts low on the left, rises exponentially to the right, and levels off. This is the signal the envelope signal converter (envelope operator) puts out, and the same symbol appears again after the mixer: the shape does not change before and after attenuation, so the block looks the same — only the size is scaled by α(x).

The exponential can be drawn four ways — up to the right, up to the left, down to the right, down to the left:

Here the four are interchangeable: we cannot control whether Tair is decaying up or down — that depends on the sign of the difference, on whether Ts > To or Ts < To — so it does not matter which way it is drawn; we prefer up to the right. But if the direction is known, the diagram can be changed slightly to draw the way it actually goes.

Mixer — circle with an ×. The product sign: two signals go in and are multiplied. In the chain the exponential Tair(t) is mixed with the attenuation of space: Tair(t) × α(x) = Tair(t) at position x. (Addition is a different circle — the addition operator, ⊕ with a +, defined beside the difference operator above, used where signals add — as TH(x) and TH(t) add in the complex diagram in section 11.)

Attenuation α(x) — circle with a Bode plot. A straight line to the right, like a low-pass filter, then a straight line angled down after the cutoff, with a dashed vertical line inside the circle where the cutoff is. This is the second input to the mixer: the attenuation of space, α(x). Filter symbols can likewise be drawn different ways — low-pass, high-pass — but here we know it is a low-pass, so this symbol is drawn accurately as one. Drawing it brings space back into the diagram — but only in this simplified α(x) form: that is simplification 7 — and drawing that attenuation as this Bode plot, rather than as the exponential itself, is simplification 8.

Bode Plot Converter — circle with a broken line. A horizontal line runs from the left to the centre, at one third down from the top; there the line breaks — nothing joins the two halves, no vertical drop is drawn — and a second horizontal line runs from the centre to the right, at one third up from the bottom: a broken line, like a broken square wave. This is the symbol of the Bode Plot Converter, a type of transform converter: it takes an exponential and makes it a linear piecewise function — here, the exponential transform of attenuation over distance goes in and the linear Bode response comes out — which gives us the simple filter model. It is the step that turns the exponential attenuation into the Bode plot drawn in the attenuation symbol above — simplification 8 — drawn as its own block in the complex diagram in section 11. In the simplified diagram it is not drawn at all: the attenuation is drawn straight away in its Bode form. That Bode form belongs to the frequency response — oscillation amplitude against frequency at a fixed depth, with skin depth δ = √(2α/ω); the spatial attenuation itself stays the exponential (section 9).

Steady-state filter — circle with an hourglass. The steady-state temporal filter: it passes only the part of the signal at and after steady state, t ≥ ts, where ts is the time it takes to reach steady state. This is simplification 9, drawn as a block: the transient is filtered out in time, and what remains is the settled value.

Constant signal — circle with a straight line. A straight line through the centre of the circle: a constant line graph. The last block of the chain — T constant at steady state at a given position x, the output of the steady-state filter.

Simplification 3 — Pad temperature constant in space: T(t, x, y) → TH(t)

Before the chain can start, the pad's temperature field T(t, x, y) is taken as constant in the spatial dimensions of the pad — the same everywhere on the pad — so the position parameters x and y are removed and what remains is TH(t), a function of time only. And even TH(t) is an oscillator: the pad does not jump to its new value, it swings and settles toward it. Space is not gone for good — it comes back later, in simplified form, as the attenuation α(x) at the mixer (simplification 7), with the heat travelling straight down only on the way (simplification 6).

Simplification 4 — TH = Ts at steady state

The heating pad temperature is set equal to the slider setting: TH = Ts, the steady-state constant value. Before that, the chain starts from two values: To, the previous steady-state value, and Ts, the new slider setting. Their difference, from the difference operator, is the signal that drives everything after it — TH starts at To and changes toward Ts based on that difference.

So, to fix the vocabulary: we have two classifications — signals and filters. Under signals, our first subclass is the oscillator — and the temperature signal here is one, its exponential form a simplification with the swing removed. Between a signal and a filter we now also have a block: the envelope signal converter (envelope operator), which turns an oscillator signal into the simplified exponential that the filter then acts on. And the heating-panel temperature distribution, modelled well as a low-pass filter, is classified here as a thermodynamic filter. Filters can also be classified as transforms — also called mixers, symbol ⊗, and after the filter we now also have a second kind of converter block: the transform converter — here the Bode Plot Converter, a type of transform converter, which turns the filter's exponential response into its linear Bode response. Like the envelope signal converter, it is named, not lettered, and drawn by its own symbol: the broken line. And in the shorthand notation defined just above, each of these is drawn as its circle: minus for the difference operator and plus (⊕, the adder) for the addition operator, sine wave for the oscillator, hash for a medium change, curved dashed diamond — its sides concave inwards — for the envelope signal converter (envelope operator), exponential curve for the exponential signal, × for the mixer, Bode plot for the attenuation α(x), broken line for the Bode Plot Converter, hourglass for the steady-state filter, and a straight line for the final constant signal.

Equations — from the simple solution to T_water

The simple solution (air only)

One temperature for the air, driven by the pad:   dT/dt = k·(Ts − T),   Ts = Theating pad

Solution:   T(t) = Ts − (Ts − T0) e−kt   (one medium, one exponential, no surface)

With water — a second temperature, T_water

The water is a second medium with its own temperature, Twater. The pad still drives the air, and now the air drives the water across the surface:

Air:   dTair/dt = kair·(Theating pad − Tair) − ks·(Tair − Twater)

Water:   dTwater/dt = kw·(Tair − Twater)   (k_w is small — water has far more heat capacity, so T_water moves slowly and lags T_air)

Steady state:   Tair = Twater = Ts,   and total water is conserved:   H2O total = H2O liquid + H2O gaseous   (sealed tank — water only moves between liquid and vapour)

The surface — saturation and the spring model

Clapeyron (Clausius–Clapeyron):   d ln psat / dT = L / (Rv T2)   (L = enthalpy of vaporisation — the energy price of breaking hydrogen bonds; the saturation point is the equilibrium point, and it moves with temperature)

Temperature → energy fluctuations at the surface:   ΔE ≈ ms cp ΔT,   and the share of surface molecules able to escape ∝ e−L / (kB T)   (a temperature fluctuation is an energy fluctuation: raise T and the Boltzmann tail fattens, more surface molecules can pay L and leave as gas; lower T and the tail thins, more land back as liquid — that exchange is what moves the surface, and in level it is tiny, of the order of micrometres, so the graphs below track amounts of H₂O, not millimetres of level)

Spring model of the surface, for the gap u from equilibrium:   d2u/dt2 + 2ζωn du/dt + ωn2 u = 0

Damped solution:   u(t) = A e−t/τ cos(ωd t + φ)   (an oscillation inside a decaying exponential envelope — keep the envelope and drop the wiggle and you are back to the simple exponential)

A terrarium with air and water in it, and a surface between them. The spring model and the oscillations are about that surface — how it is disturbed when the temperature changes, and how it settles again at the new equilibrium.

1. Three mediums now — pad, air, water

The tank now has water in it, filled up to the water level, with air above it. Heat now travels through three mediums in a chain: the heating pad hands heat to the air, and the air hands heat to the water across the surface. That is why the heat distribution had to be redone — the animation and the second graph above show one temperature profile running through all three, and the water part behaves differently: water has a much larger heat capacity, so it warms and cools slowly, and it evens the temperature out with depth instead of letting it fade quickly the way air does.

The tank is sealed and the water level is steady in the long run: H2O total = H2O liquid + H2O gaseous. Water is never created or lost — it only moves between the liquid and the vapour in the air. The point where the two balance is the saturation point, and that saturation point is the equilibrium point of this system.

2. The step from the simple solution to T_water

Start from the simple solution: one temperature for the air, and two named temperatures in the story — Ts, the setpoint, which is Theating pad, the pad temperature the thermostat holds. One equation, one exponential.

Adding water means adding one more temperature: Twater. Nothing about the pad or the air equation is thrown away — the air equation gains one extra term, the heat it loses to (or gains from) the water, and the water gets its own equation, driven by the gap (Tair − Twater). Because water's heat capacity is so much bigger, its constant kw is small: Twater chases Tair slowly. That lag is exactly what the live graph shows — move the slider and Tair moves first, Twater follows behind. In the steady state the chase ends: Tair = Twater = Ts. When the temperature changes, the system passes through a transient — water temporarily evaporates or condenses more — until it reaches the new equilibrium at the new saturation point.

3. The water surface and hydrogen bonds

The liquid itself is mostly still. Almost everything happens at the surface, where molecules constantly leave into the air (evaporation) and land back (condensation). To see why the surface behaves as it does, look at how water molecules connect.

Molecules at the surface, magnified. The dashed hydrogen bonds join the hydrogen of one molecule to the oxygen of its neighbour.

What a hydrogen bond is. Inside one molecule, the two hydrogens are strongly bonded to their own oxygen — those covalent bonds do not come apart. A hydrogen bond happens between molecules, and it is much weaker. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the oxygen end of a molecule is slightly negative and each hydrogen end slightly positive: every water molecule is a tiny dipole, a little magnet-like object with a positive and a negative end. The slightly positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen of its neighbour — that attraction is a hydrogen bond.

Hydrogen bonds are only about a twentieth as strong as the covalent bonds inside the molecule, so the thermal jiggling that is temperature breaks them constantly, and new ones form a moment later with whichever neighbour is close. Liquid water is a flickering network of little attractions that stretch, snap and reconnect. Those bonds are also why water holds together at all — cohesion: to leave the liquid, a molecule must break its hydrogen bonds, and that costs energy. At the surface there are no neighbours above, so a surface molecule's bonds pull it only sideways and down, back into the liquid — surface tension, a surface always being pulled inward, like a stretched skin. That is why we can ignore the mostly-still bulk and focus on the surface: it is the only unbalanced place, and the only place molecules leave or arrive.

4. The missing step — temperature → energy fluctuations at the surface

One step was missing between the temperature slider and the moving surface, and it is this conversion. A temperature is an average particle energy, so a temperature fluctuation is an energy fluctuation — at the surface, ΔE ≈ ms cp ΔT for the surface layer. That energy is what the surface spends and receives: a surface molecule can only leave the liquid if its share of the energy is enough to break its hydrogen bonds — the price L in the Clapeyron equation — and the share of molecules rich enough sits in the tail of the Boltzmann distribution, ∝ e−L/(kB T).

So when the temperature wobbles or steps, the energy at the surface wobbles with it: the tail fattens, evaporation wins for a while and the surface is disturbed one way; the tail thins, condensation wins and it is disturbed the other way. Those energy fluctuations at the surface are what make the water level oscillate through the transient, and what carry it to the new steady state — the new saturation point — once the fluctuations settle at the new temperature. Averaged, that disturbed surface is the spring of the next section.

One honest warning about size: converted into a distance, this is tiny. The vapour the air above the water can hold is only grams, spread over the whole surface, so the level itself moves by micrometres, not millimetres. The spring picture is still the right picture — just do not expect to see the level travel. That is why the H2O graph above and the partition spring in section 7 track amounts of water, liquid and gas, rather than a distance.

5. From Clapeyron to the spring

The Clapeyron equation describes the equilibrium between the liquid and its vapour: at any temperature the vapour settles at one saturation pressure. Below it, more molecules leave than land — evaporation. Above it, more land than leave — condensation. Its Clausius–Clapeyron form (in the equations above) involves exactly the quantities you would expect: enthalpy (through L, the energy price of breaking the hydrogen bonds), entropy folded into the same balance, volume change, pressure and temperature. Its message: change the temperature and you move the equilibrium target — the saturation point itself shifts.

Now the spring. Each hydrogen bond is a tiny attraction that pulls back when stretched — a tiny spring. Millions acting together give the surface as a whole a restoring pull, roughly proportional to how far it is disturbed from equilibrium. So we replace the whole flickering network with one spring standing in for the surface: Clapeyron sets where the spring wants to rest; the bond network supplies the pull.

It is a reverse spring — pulled, not pushed. Set the temperature below where it currently is, and the spring's target is set below where the surface currently sits: the spring feels a pull inward. Set it above, and it is pulled outward. The temperature difference changes first; the pull follows. At particle level the swinging dies because particles hit other particles, lose energy, bounce back, bounce the other way and lose more — until they reach the new average.

7. H₂O total = H₂O liquid + H₂O gas — the partition spring

Now run the same spring idea not on the water level, but on the split of the water itself. The tank is sealed, so the total never changes — it only divides: H2O total = H2O liquid + H2O gas. Temperature sets the target split, through the saturation point: warm the tank and the equilibrium gas share grows while the liquid share falls by the same amount; cool it and the split swings back. But the split does not jump — evaporation and condensation overshoot each other in turn, so the gas share oscillates around its new target inside the same decaying envelope, exactly like the surface did. One spring, this time standing in for the partition instead of the level.

This is the complete model for the water level in a container, based on temperature alone. The whole chain, in order: temperature changes the saturation point → the saturation point is the equilibrium point → when the temperature changes, the equilibrium itself changes → the transient is simply the system travelling to the new equilibrium. Nothing else is needed: give the model a temperature and it gives you where the water must end up; give it a temperature change and the spring gives you the journey — the overshoot, the settling, the new steady state. The water level is not a separate thing to solve afterwards; it is this partition, read at the surface.

It also ties back to the temperature distribution. The split is not set by some single tank temperature — it is set at the surface, by the temperature the distribution graph (second graph at the top of this tab) has at the water-level depth. That surface temperature sets the local saturation point there: how much vapour the air right above the surface can hold. Read the distribution to get the surface temperature, the surface temperature gives the saturation point, the saturation point gives the target split — and the two overlaid curves, here and in the graph at the top of this tab, then let you read off, at any given time, how much water is in the air as gas and how much is in the liquid, mid-transient or settled, with the dashed total confirming the sum never changes.

Move the temperature slider — here, or the one at the top of this tab. The dashed line on the spring jumps to the new target gas share, the spring oscillates toward it, and the graph on the right is drawn live from that motion: H2O gas climbing or falling in swings, H2O liquid mirroring it exactly, and H2O total as the flat dashed line at 100% — the sum never moves, only the split does.

8. Temperature drives, the amounts accumulate — driver and integral

The two live graphs on this tab are not two separate stories — one is the cause, the other is the accumulated effect. The temperature graph is the driver. The liquid/gas graph is its integral.

Temperature is energy — the average energy of the particles — and a temperature difference is a rate waiting to happen. Everywhere in this model, a flow is proportional to a gap: heat flow ∝ (Ts − T), and dT/dt = k·(gap). So read the temperature graph as a graph of driving force over time: the vertical distance between Tair and Twater at any moment is the push acting at that moment. A wide gap means change is being driven hard; a narrow gap means it is barely being driven at all. That is also why the auto-zoom on that graph matters — as the two curves converge the push fades, and the zoom keeps the small remaining push visible instead of letting it flatten into the axis.

The push acts at the surface, and the surface is where the two graphs meet. The water temperature at the surface sets the saturation point there — through Clapeyron, a warmer surface has a higher saturation pressure — and the saturation point sets how much water the air holds at equilibrium: the target gas share on the H2O graph. The air temperature controls the supply side: it decides how fast energy arrives at the surface, to pay the hydrogen-bond price L for molecules leaving, or how fast energy is carried away when molecules land back and give that price up. Twater names the target; Tair names how fast energy is being delivered toward it.

H2O gas(t) = H2O gas(0) + ∫0t (evaporation − condensation) dt,   with   (evaporation − condensation) ∝ the driving gap

The amounts do not respond to the temperature itself — they respond to the net exchange rate, summed over time. While Tair and Twater differ, the gap is not zero, evaporation and condensation do not balance, and the integral keeps accumulating: every second of gap adds a little more water to the air, or takes a little back, so the liquid and gas curves keep moving. The total moved is the area under the gap curve, not just its height — a short, wide gap and a long, narrow gap can accumulate exactly the same change.

When the temperatures converge — Tair = Twater = Ts, the oscillating curves on the zoomed graph settling onto each other — the gap goes to zero, evaporation balances condensation, and the integrand switches off. The integral stops accumulating, so the partition settles where it is: at the split belonging to that temperature, the one the equilibrium graph at the top of this tab reads off. Change the temperature again and the whole sequence restarts: new surface temperature, new saturation point, new target split — and a fresh gap on the temperature graph driving a fresh stretch of accumulation on the amounts graph.

So read the two graphs in that order. Temperature graph first: how big is the gap, which way does it point, how fast is it closing — the driver, in degrees, right now. Liquid/gas graph second: how much that driver has added up to so far — the integral, in percent, over the whole history. The driver can be back near zero while the amounts are still far from where they started, because the integral remembers everything the gap has done.

9. Signals, filters, and simplifications — the vocabulary in this system

The same two classifications used for system diagrams apply here: signals and filters. The definitions below are the vocabulary this note concludes with — the same words, carried into this system.

Signal — a quantity that carries change through a system: it has a value that varies — with time, with position, or with both — and a system diagram is, in large part, a picture of what happens to its signals. In this system the signals are Tair, Twater, the gap of the surface from its equilibrium, and the two amounts, H2O liquid and H2O gas. Signals are classified by the shape of that variation.

Filter — a part of a system that a signal passes through and is changed by: part of the signal gets through, part is attenuated or held back, so the signal that comes out is a reshaped version of the signal that went in. A filter is classified by what it lets through and what it cuts off, and by where its cutoff point falls.

Thermodynamic filter — our name, for system diagrams, for a filter whose filtering is done by a thermodynamic process rather than by an electronic component. In this system it is distance through the air, and then through the water, acting on temperature: the temperature signal leaves the panel at full strength, passes nearly whole near the top, breaks at the cutoff point, and is attenuated with distance below it — through the surface and down through the water as well as through the air above it. In electronics language it is modelled well as a low-pass filter; in our classification it is a thermodynamic filter.

Oscillator — the first subclass of signal. An oscillator is a signal that swings around a target or equilibrium value — overshooting it, coming back, and, if it is damped, settling onto it in swings that get smaller. That is the underlying kind of signal in this system: Tair and Twater swing around Ts, the surface swings around the water level for the new saturation point, and the liquid/gas split swings around its target split. The spring model produces an oscillator: a mechanical spring is the parallel that got us there, and a capacitor charging and discharging is an electrical parallel for the same kind of settling behaviour, but the classification names the signal, not the parallel. Many of the signals we will deal with will be oscillators.

Exponential, linear and constant signals here are simplifications of oscillator signals. Several of the signals drawn and written in this note are shown in a simpler shape than the oscillator underneath — and in every case the simpler shape is got by removing part of the oscillator, not by the system changing kind:

Exponential — the envelope with the swing removed. The white dashed Newton exponentials behind the live Tair / Twater graph, and the simple solution T(t) = Ts − (Ts − T0) e−kt, are simplifications. The full damped response is u(t) = A e−t/τ cos(ωd t + φ) — an oscillation inside a decaying envelope. Keep the envelope e−t/τ and drop the cos term — the swing — and what is left is the exponential. The exponential is the oscillator with its wiggle deleted.

Constant — the settled limit with the travel removed. A signal drawn as constant — flat at its steady-state value, the target line at Ts, or the liquid/gas split drawn at its equilibrium value for each temperature in the H2O graph at the top of this tab — is a simplification. It shows only where the oscillator ends up, after its swings have died away, and removes the travel: the overshoot and settling by which the surface and the partition actually get there. The flat dashed H2O total line is exact for a sealed tank — the total really does not change — but it must not be read as the components being constant: H2O liquid and H2O gas underneath it are oscillators, mirroring each other around their target split while their sum stays still.

Linear — the straight-line approximation with the swing removed. Where a relation here is written as linear — a flow proportional to a gap, dT/dt = k·(gap), or the spring's restoring pull taken as proportional to how far the surface is disturbed — that straight-line form is a simplification. It is a good local approximation to the push acting at one moment, but the signal that push produces over time is not a straight-line journey to the target: driven by that push, the surface and the temperatures overshoot, come back, and settle — an oscillator again.

Linear signal — for contrast, a signal whose variation really is proportional and straight-line — double the input and you double the change, with no swing around a target and no cutoff. That is a different underlying kind, and the diagram should say so when it is dealing with one. The signals in this system are not that kind: when one of them is shown as linear, it is the approximation that is linear, not the signal.

So, to fix the vocabulary here as well: we have two classifications — signals and filters. Under signals, our first subclass is the oscillator, and the main signals of this system — the two temperatures, the surface, and the liquid/gas split — are oscillators. The temperature distribution through the air and the water, modelled well as a low-pass filter, is classified here as a thermodynamic filter. And any signal in this note shown as exponential, linear or constant is a simplification of an underlying oscillator signal: the exponential keeps its envelope, the constant keeps its settled value, the linear keeps its local push — each one leaves the swing out.
